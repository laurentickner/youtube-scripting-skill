## 2026-06-XX (b) — follow-up review calls (coach: Kyle Denyssen) — DM-sales scripts review

Kyle reviewing Lauren's revised DM-sales scripts. New refinements on top of the earlier entries:

### Don't announce the next sub-point — dangle the carrot
When a body point has N sub-points, preface that there ARE N (open the loop), but do NOT name sub-point 2 while teaching sub-point 1. Naming what's coming kills retention. Teach point 1 fully, handle the "duh" objection ("and you're probably thinking, well, duh — but let me explain"), THEN reveal point 2. *"Dangle the carrot in front of their face."*

### Open the framework loop early, THEN explain definitions
If you must explain definitions/context before the framework the viewer came for, open the loop first: *"before I get into the [N-step process], you first need to understand [X]. Hold on with me, then we'll get to all the juicy stuff."* Sophisticated audiences especially want the tangible framework teased up front. Don't bury the framework behind unannounced definitions.

### Tie the body opening back to the title/intro
Open the body by referencing the outcome promised in the title/intro ("so you know exactly how to [title outcome]"), not a cold start into content.

### Gold-vein topic — milk it (Luis's term)
Once you find a topic that performs (across any platform), keep making content on it. For Lauren, DM sales / booking calls / closing in DMs is the gold vein — it works on every platform, so double down. *"As soon as you find a gold vein topic, literally milk it."*

### Iman's packaging-theft process (how the team finds video ideas)
Iman finds channels with the same demographic/audience, finds their 1M+ view videos, and steals the PACKAGING (title) — proven packaging. Then watches those videos, copies the STRUCTURE, and writes his own copy. *"Copy what's proven to work."* Use this to ideate when struggling with views: find videos already doing well from similar-niche creators, copy the structure, make it your own. (Note: structure/packaging modelling — original copy, not verbatim.)

### Reuse a viral LinkedIn hook as the YouTube hook
If a LinkedIn post went viral on a topic, reuse its exact hook as the video's hook. Lauren's "I've closed deals as big as $100k with zero sales calls" LinkedIn hook → use verbatim as the video hook, then build credibility from there. Stronger + already-validated.

### Reduce-cognitive-load = the whole game (re-confirmed)
Across both review calls, the single recurring fix was: preface + package clearly + hold their hand, rather than dumping. *"The thing that solves cognitive load is prefacing and the way you package it and hold the hand through it. Rather than just dumping."* Scripts are "very valuable, explained well — it's just getting the structure right."

### Kyle's tools (re-confirmed): Notion for scripting, Natural Reader (TTS) for reviewing scripts faster.

### Universal rule: cognitive load = prefacing failure

Kyle verbatim: *"The thing that solves cognitive load is prefacing and the way you package it and hold the hand through it. Rather than just dumping."*

This is the single most important script principle in the whole skill. If a section feels muddy, the fix is almost always **preface earlier and harder** — not cut content.

### Body-point structure (clarified)

- **The OVERARCHING body point uses ONE framework** — pick Value Loop (Callaway) OR Foundation/Theory/Example/Practice (Luis/Brazilian-paid-event-creator). Different body points can use different frameworks.
- **SUB-points inside a body point must follow a CONSISTENT mini-framework.** If body point A has 3 sub-points, all 3 follow the same shape (e.g. state-the-point / mini-example / mini-action-item).
- **You CAN mix frameworks across body points** — what matters is that each individual body point + its sub-points are internally consistent.

### Prefacing sub-points (the #1 cognitive-load fix)

When a body point contains multiple sub-points, you MUST preface them before walking through. Pattern:

> *"There are [N] things missing in her positioning. They are [A], [B], [C]. Let's go through each."*

Then walk through them, in that order. **Never blabber through sub-points without prefacing.** Without the preface, the viewer is trying to figure out structure mid-explanation, which is the cognitive-load failure.

Lauren's $5k script failure: in the positioning body point she jumped from price → testimonials → referrals without stating "positioning has three sub-things." Kyle's fix: name positioning, state the 3 sub-things upfront, then walk through.

### State the PRINCIPLE first, not the surface habit

Lauren's mistake: *"Let's start with the one anyone charging under $5,000 is doing wrong. They're selling what they do."*

Kyle's correction: name the principle directly. The principle is **POSITIONING**. Then explain what positioning means using the surface habit ("describing what you do at surface level") as the illustration.

Pattern:
> *"Let's start with the first thing anyone charging under $5,000 is doing wrong. It comes down to one thing: positioning. Whenever you describe what you do, you describe yourself at surface level. Let me explain..."*

The principle is the headline. The surface-habit description is the explanation.

### Drop redundant analogies when an example does the same work

Lauren had a flat-screen TV analogy AND the Meena Samara example, both illustrating the same point (commoditisation → price compression). Kyle: cut the TV analogy, go straight to Meena. Two illustrations of the same point doubles cognitive load and weakens both.

**Rule:** if your example does the teaching, drop the analogy. If your analogy is doing the teaching and the example is just proof, keep the analogy and use the example as proof.

### Body-point ordering — Callaway rule

Kyle learnt this from Callaway. **NEVER put your most valuable body point first.**

- **Body point 1 = SECOND most valuable** — sets a baseline of "this is good."
- **Body point 2 = MOST valuable** — creates "this is even BETTER, keep watching" pattern.
- **Body points 3-N** = structure as you please.

Logic: viewers see point 1 (good), then point 2 (even better), then assume the pattern continues, so they stay. If you front-load the best point, everything after feels like a letdown.

Application to current scripts: re-audit body order. Move the most valuable point to SLOT 2, not SLOT 1.

### "Cool-name the framework" — naming convention

Kyle: don't say "four things." Say *"four cardinal rules."* Give the framework a memorable name.

Other examples Kyle/team use: "Value Ladder," "Lazy Model Criteria," "8 Stages of Toxic Culture." The cool name becomes the memorable container the viewer walks away with.

For Lauren's $5k script: "Four Cardinal Rules" — locked.

### When a body point is too dense

Lauren's 4th body point on the $5k script = 9-step sales process. Too dense for the whole video.

Three solutions Kyle / Antonio offered:
1. **Condense to phases.** 9 steps → 4 phases (qualifying / nurturing / selling / onboarding).
2. **Make it its own video.** CTA at the end: *"This is a high-level overview. If you want the full breakdown, I made a whole video on it — link below."*
3. **Both** — condense to phases in this video + lead magnet / CTA to standalone video.

Iman pattern Kyle referenced: *"I only have you for so long, and your attention spans are only so short, so this is a high-level overview. If you really want to know in depth, [lead magnet / next video]."*

### Open loop in intro for the MOST valuable body point

When body point N is the most valuable AND most dense, open a loop in the intro to prime focus:

> *"The fourth one is the most valuable and the one I spent the most time on. Make sure you set aside time to actually listen when we get there, because it's going to make the biggest impact."*

This is "anti-distraction priming" — call them out on their tendency to half-watch, force them to mentally commit to focusing when the key beat lands. Also accommodates viewers who skip ahead — they arrive in focus mode.

### Step-by-step body points DON'T need unified mini-framework

A genuinely step-by-step body point (e.g. 9-step sales process) doesn't need each step to follow the same mini-framework. The sequence IS the structure.

What it DOES need: clear visuals on screen for each step. Editor's job. Don't try to over-package what's naturally sequential.

### Soft CTAs — content angle worth teaching

Antonio's add (which Lauren confirmed): teach WHY soft CTAs have lower sales resistance. Viewers know hard CTAs = ManyChat + setter follow-up = sales process. Soft CTAs feel like helpful resource-sharing.

Pattern Lauren can use: *"The reason this works is hard CTAs are pattern-matched to sales pipelines now. Soft CTAs aren't. They land as 'this person is being helpful' not 'this person is selling.'"*

### Process recap (Kyle's verbatim workflow)

1. Receive brief — title alone, or title + write-up, or title + meeting transcript.
2. Brain dump.
3. Build the OVERALL packaging (the framework name + structure for the whole video).
4. Build the PER-POINT packaging (the framework for each body point).
5. Write body points (in order, applying value-loop or foundation/theory/example/practice).
6. **Write the INTRO LAST.** Always.
7. The intro always has: Hook + Credibility + Present Packaging (= Preview, same thing).

### Terminology lock

**Preview = Present Packaging.** Same beat. Kyle uses both terms interchangeably. The skill should treat them as one beat.

### Intros are always the weakest beat

Kyle's verbatim: *"introductions are going to need work, but I think they always need work."* Intros are NEVER nailed first-pass. Plan for 2-3 iterations.

### Tools Kyle uses

- **Notion** for scripting (prefers over Google Docs, "easier to segment thoughts")
- **Natural Reader** (text-to-speech) for reviewing scripts faster

Kyle going to Iceland for ~2 weeks. Lauren ships next script after he's back. No review calls during his trip.

## 2026-06-01 — live call (coach: Kyle Denyssen, with Antonio Amaral on attendance)

### Two intro structures — pick by production format

Kyle drew an explicit fork in intro structure based on format:

**Long Form (Structure 1, the existing default):** Hook → Break Belief → X2X → Credibility → Preview.
- Use for: off-the-cuff / car format / low production / narrative installation / middle-of-funnel content for existing audience / sophisticated audiences where belief-breaking takes time.
- *"Personally, when we do videos that are sort of that give the off-the-cuff sort of vibe, that aren't really high production, those I'll usually use something a little bit more drawn out, because there's an agenda, of course, that we really need to get across."*

**Short Form (NEW — high-production reach play):** Hook → Preview → X2X → Credibility.
- Use for: high-production sit-down videos / chasing reach / top-of-funnel.
- Skip Break Belief entirely. Move Preview up to slot 2 so expectations are met as fast as possible.
- Length target: ~15–20 seconds total.
- *"Intro, preview, X to X, credibility, getting into it. So that's usually quite quick as well. That takes like, I would say 15 to 20 seconds in an intro that usually works as well."*
- Logic: *"We want to meet those expectations quickly. And we're going for reach. By giving the preview, we're meeting those expectations, and have the chance to exceed them when we give the preview."*

**The decision rule:** ask "is this a sit-down high-production video for reach, or off-the-cuff narrative installation?" If sit-down/reach → Short Form. If car/narrative → Long Form.

### Cognitive load rule (universal, mandatory)

*"The second you say something that's not explained well, or it's sort of just jumps out of them, like the phantom sales team, I was immediately like, oh, what's that? And then it wasn't explained. And then I was fixated on that. And then I was confused, because I must, the rest of it."*

- **Do NOT name a system / framework / branded term in the intro that you haven't yet explained.** Naming triggers "what's that?" — the viewer fixates and stops processing the rest.
- Specific example from the call: "Phantom Sales Team" should NOT appear in the intro for the AI sales video. The viewer learns the name LATER in the script when the system is being explained.
- Universal rule from Kyle: *"You almost have to treat it like you're speaking to a child in order. Otherwise, as soon as there's that little bit of effort that they have to have on their end, they're going to click off, or they get confused."*
- This applies even (especially) to sophisticated audiences. Sophistication does NOT mean tolerate higher cognitive load. It means deliver clarity without dumbing down content.

### Iman's hook preferences (Kyle's observed pattern)

Kyle admits hooks are his weakest area — Iman almost always rewrites them. Observed pattern of what Iman changes:

1. **Shorter** than what Kyle wrote.
2. **Hard hitting.** Something that lands immediately.
3. **Industry stat / fact / interesting number as the first line.** Hooks anchored in concrete numbers about market size / growth rate / industry shifts.
4. **Flows nicely into what comes next** — hook ≠ standalone clever line. It has to bridge into the Preview.

*"It's literally as short as possible, and as hard hitting as possible. And but then again, something that flows nicely into what comes next, because it has to flow. If it doesn't flow, then it's not a good script to me."*

Action: Lauren's current Phantom Sales Team intro hook (78 words ≈ 1.3 min) is way too long. For high-production reach videos, target a much shorter, stat-led hook.

### Trending-title intro pattern (mandatory for "X with AI it feels illegal"-style titles)

When using a trending / templated title, the intro MUST mirror the title and immediately bridge to mechanism. Kyle's verbatim template:

> *"In today's video, I'm going to show you exactly [title promise — e.g. 'how to get so many high-ticket coaching clients with AI, it feels illegal']. And the way I'm going to do that is by [mechanism preview — e.g. 'showing you seven tools that replaces your sales team and increases the efficiency by X percentage']."*

The "And the way I'm going to do that is by..." sentence IS the Preview beat. Don't skip it. Don't try to be clever about it.

### X to X = name the destination

After Preview lands, X to X = explicitly name the destination state the viewer reaches.

- For Lauren: *"this is going to get you from where you are to hitting your first hundred K a month"* — even when the actual video teaches a $20k/m mechanism. Sell what they want, give them what they need.
- *"Sell them what they want, give them what they need."*

### Punchy ≠ AI-punchy

- **Punchy + flowing = good.** Punchy because every word earns its place; flowing because sentences connect into each other.
- **AI-punchy = banned.** *"Three words, full stop. Three words, full stop."* Staccato-rhythm fake-punchy is the AI tell. Read aloud test: would I naturally say this when not reading?

### Peeling onion — setup before point (the #1 fix for sophisticated audiences)

The biggest structural problem Kyle identified across Lauren's scripts: she gets to points too quickly, without setup. Points don't land because there's no priming.

- Kyle's prompt to Claude when scripting: *"explain this in a way that it's almost like peeling back layers of an onion to get to a point."*
- Take TIME with the setup. Use analogies. The point is the resolution; the setup is what makes the resolution feel earned.
- **Worked example Kyle gave (from his teacher Luis):** a life insurance salesman who didn't lead with "buy life insurance." Instead: *"I want you to view yourself like a machine that pays 5k every single month. Would you ensure that machine? Of course you would ensure that machine. You are that machine for your family."* The point ("buy life insurance") only lands because of the setup ("you are the machine").
- Lauren's current failure mode: jumps to the point because she thinks setup is wasting time. Sophisticated audiences need LESS fluff, not LESS setup. Distinguish the two.

### Kyle's hierarchy of what matters in a script (verbatim)

When asked what he focuses on when reviewing scripts:

2. **Flow between points** — chronological + sequential.

4. **Does it sound natural** — no trace of AI in the read-aloud.
5. **Only after these are right** — layer in narratives / unique mechanisms / specific positioning.

*"Only after I've got that right, do I layer in anything else."*

## 2026-05-24 — async Notion review (coach: Kyle Denyssen, Educate.io team)

**Reviewed:** Full script of *"the EXACT path to $20,000/m as an online coach (no ads, no funnels)"* — intro packaging + body point 1 (Clients) reviewed in detail; framework given for body points 2–5. Delivered as inline Notion comments + Slack message at 2:21am: *"Most of my comments and rewrites are concentrated on the intro packaging and body point 1, but the framework I used to restructure body point 1 is meant to apply to all the other body points just as much."*

### Intro / packaging
- **Use Structure 1 for intros of long-form scripts.** Hook → Break Belief → X2X (Bridge) → Credibility → Preview. *"Structure 1 would be best to follow, as it's much faster to execute, usually takes around 30 seconds. Given this is a longer script overall, I'd want to get into the content as fast as possible. This is great for retention and hits on all elements that are important in an introduction."*
- **Curiosity-gap rule for acronym frameworks: do NOT reveal each element of the acronym in the intro.** Only introduce the framework name. *"I wouldn't reveal each element. Not revealing each element we create a curiosity gab/open loop which helps retention."* Each letter then gets revealed at the moment its body beat lands.
- **If your body teaches the acronym out of left-to-right order, call out the reverse order explicitly at the top of the first body beat.** *"The acronym is SCALE, so the viewer expects you to start with S (Sales). But you start with C (Clients). Worth calling out the reverse order explicitly before you dive in, otherwise the viewer is left trying to figure out why on their own."* Don't make the viewer do that work — name the swap and the reason.
- **Negative-curiosity retention loop — end the intro on a *what-it-isn't* tease, not the named pillar.** Instead of *"the one most people skip is Economics"* (names the pillar = partial closure), end with *"the most important of the five, the one almost nobody is paying attention to, has nothing to do with getting more leads."* Names what it *isn't* (Leads), keeps what it *is* hidden. Trade-off: stronger surprise when the pillar lands vs weaker mid-video pull. Use the negative version when you're confident in retention through the body; use the named version when you need a specific hook to pull viewers forward.
- **Sympathy wedge beats hard industry attack for the Break Belief beat.** *"It's not your fault, you've just been misled"* lands softer and more conversion-friendly than *"the gurus are wrong and that's why you're stuck."* The viewer doesn't have to defend their past choices to keep watching. Pattern: *"if you've tried [common advice] and you're still stuck, it's not your fault — you've just been misled. The coaches who scale past that are not doing X, Y, Z. They've done the opposite."* Especially powerful when the audience has personally tried the conventional advice.
- **Time-budget bridge — name the runtime in the X2X beat.** *"In the next 30 minutes I'm going to walk you through every one of those five elements, so that just by following them, you can start scaling past that same number consistently."* Naming the exact runtime sets the viewer's mental budget, locks the time commitment, and pairs with the SCALE preview to define exactly what they're going to get. Don't lie — match the actual video length.

### The analogy test (universal rule)
- **Every analogy in the script must be immediately understood with no effort.** *"When something doesn't click for someone watching, it creates cognitive load, which means they have to stop and think, and if they have to think, unfortunately they're probably going to click off. You want the analogy to be so easy to understand that they immediately understand it, with no effort."* If a stranger needs to read or hear it twice to get it, cut it. Failure: Lauren's migraine-pill / ibuprofen analogy. Success: athletes-with-same-coach, doctor-vs-surgeon, plumbing leak.

### Body 1 (criteria-list bodies) — STATE / COST / APPLICATION / RULE
Kyle's verbatim canonical structure for any body point that teaches a criteria list:
- **State:** name the criterion in one sentence.
- **Cost:** tell a personal story or specific client example that shows what happens when this filter gets missed. *"If you don't have a personal example for one, replace it with an analogy."*
- **Application:** how can they apply this right now in their coaching business?
- **Rule:** one line that ties it all up, and puts a pin in it before moving on to the next one.

Apply uniformly across all N criteria so the structural repetition lets the viewer ride the pattern. Pair with a visual scoreboard (e.g. a 5-pointed star that fills one point per RULE). Full framework + worked example + quality gate: `reference/state-cost-application-rule-framework.md`.

### Setup-Framework Alignment (structural rule for criteria bodies)
- **The wedge you open the body with MUST logically lead to ALL N criteria.** Lauren's v6 teed up "be a specialist" but only 2 of 5 criteria were about specialisation; Kyle flagged: *"the setup and the framework are teaching different things, which is why the section feels off."* Fix: rewrite the wedge.
- **The universal wedge Kyle uses for criteria bodies = two-kinds-of-X opener.** *"There are two kinds of [thing]. One [behaves well + grows you]. The other [behaves badly + shrinks you]. They look identical on paper. The N criteria are how you tell them apart before you say yes."* Substitutable: client, hire, prospect, partner, deal, sales call, ad creative. The criteria then become a diagnostic for telling good vs bad apart, not a list of independent lessons.

### Growth-Dynamics Logic (for two-kinds-of-X opener)
- **Make the growth-dynamic explicit, not just the symptoms.** Kyle: *"One pays you well, does the work, gets results, sends referrals (and thus grows your coaching business because you get good case studies and referrals). The other drains your energy, fights every step, churns, and tells people you didn't deliver (thus makes it more difficult to grow your coaching business)."* The invisible-loss frame (bookings you never got, referrals that never came) is what makes the rule land.

### Personal story or analogy per criterion (no naked criteria)
- **Every criterion in a list must have a story or analogy in its COST beat.** *"I like how you weaved in a personal story here to illustrate the cost of not following the rule — I recommend doing this for every criteria. If you don't have a personal example for one, replace it with an analogy."* A bare assertion (criterion + rule) won't land. Show the wreckage.

### Application advice was missing across body 1
- **Every criterion also needs an Application beat.** Kyle: *"this sub point, and the others is missing some application advice."* Tactical, prescriptive, immediate — *"on the call, ask this one question"* / *"write your offer in this one sentence."* The viewer should know exactly what to do tonight.

### Calling out objections inside the section (loops + payoffs)
- **Surface the viewer's own objection as they're thinking it, then open a loop and pay it off later.** Kyle praised Lauren's *"I know how that sounds if your calendar is not full yet"* line: *"I like this — calling out their objection as they think of it, perfect opportunity to open a loop and tease a payoff."* Pattern: name the objection → promise the payoff is coming → resolve it inside the same beat.

### Body 2-5 framework: Foundation → Theory → Example → Application
For non-criteria body points (single-concept teach), use a 4-part structure:
1. **Foundation: the 'why'** — sets context, creates urgency or curiosity.
2. **Theory: the 'what'** — the concept explained clearly.
3. **Example: the 'how it turned out'** — a real case with a real client result.
4. **Application: how can they apply it to their situation.**

Kyle: *"The content in each of them is valuable, it just needs to be structured in a way that is easy for the viewer to follow."* Use one real client per body point; if you have multiple proof points, pick the strongest as the Example and demote the rest to one-line mentions.

## 2026-05-21 — coaching call (coach: Luis, Educate.io team)

**Reviewed:** Lauren's intro / hook options for the "$20k/month as an online coach (no ads, no funnels)" video. Channel context: rebuilding a dormant ~40k-sub channel (previously a fitness / vlog channel with no real strategy).

### Intros / hooks / first 30 seconds
- **Intro stays under 30 seconds**, structured: validate the click → authority → mechanism. *"validate the click fast and then go into authority."*
- **The intro must pay off the thumbnail and packaging** — never introduce a new idea the packaging did not promise. *"It needs to go back to what's on the thumbnail."*
- **Lead with the mechanism of the PROBLEM before the mechanism of the solution.** Name the underlying problem, give it a label, open a loop — then the solution lands. *"I would lead with mechanism problem."* Lauren was opening straight on "the Scale Equation" (the solution); the coach said walk the problem first.
- **Reference to study:** Ryan Deiss's "why you struggle to scale your business" intro/VSL — a problem-mechanism intro done well.

### Authority
- **Do not claim authority with a generic monthly-revenue number** ("$20k a month") — too many people say it, it does not separate you. Use a unique, specific, more impressive angle from Lauren's actual track record.
- Coach's rewrite: "$20k a month" → ***"I have over a thousand high-ticket coaching clients"*** — *"that sounds way more impressive than 20K a month."*

### Titles / packaging
- **Broad vs specific is a funnel decision, not a fixed rule.** Paid-traffic VSL → very specific title (fewer, hotter leads). Event / challenge funnel → broader title (more leads, less committed). *"It's all about context."*

### Script structure & CTAs
- **Frame insights as "most people think X, but actually Y, in fact Z."** That structure creates the "aha" moments that drive conversion. *"that's when you get those inside moments which is what make people convert."*
- **Ask for the desired action multiple times**, and seed the CTA early — not only in the final video. *"The amount of conversions is directly related to how many times you ask."*
- **Use a casual, low-pressure, sideways CTA.** Modelled example: *"in case you want to just relax and enjoy this... we have nothing to sell you."*
- **Frame the offer as win-win** — you only profit when the viewer profits.

### Channel positioning & audience
- **Define exactly who the video targets before scripting** — cold viewers vs existing audience. Be strategic, not vlog-style.
- **Address the dominant trend (AI) rather than ignore it** — videos that name it get more views. You may argue *against* it, but do not pretend it is not there. *"whenever we do talk about it we get more views."*

### Counter-intuitive / overrides
- **"Lead with the problem" is audience-dependent, not absolute.** Problem-first suits sophisticated, aware audiences; less-aware audiences need opportunity-first — sell the opportunity before the problem even exists. Calibrate to the market's sophistication level.
- **Do not filter "broke" viewers out of the funnel.** Low-budget viewers still lift reach and recommendations; qualify for buyers through ad copy, not by repelling registrations.
