# STATE / COST / APPLICATION / RULE — Criteria-Body Framework

Canonical 4-part structure for body points that teach a **criteria list** (N things to check, N signs of X, N rules of Y, N ways to spot Z). Source: Kyle Denyssen (Educate.io scripting coach), async Notion review of Lauren Tickner's *"the EXACT path to $20,000/m as an online coach"* script, 2026-05-24.

> Use this whenever the body point's underlying job is *"here's a list of N criteria — go through each."* For non-criteria body points (single concept teach, single client transformation, single mechanism explanation), use the Foundation → Theory → Example → Application framework from `coaching-feedback.md` 2026-05-21 instead.

---

## 1. The 4-part structure (Kyle's verbatim definitions)

For each criterion in your list, run all four beats, in this order:

### a. STATE
> *"Name the criterion in one sentence."*

Short. Declarative. The viewer should know exactly what filter is being introduced before you start unpacking it. Example: *"They have to be coachable."*

### b. COST
> *"Tell a personal story or specific client example that shows what happens when this filter gets missed. If you don't have a personal example for one, replace it with an analogy."*

This is where the criterion earns the viewer's emotional buy-in. The pattern is **failure-by-example** — show the wreckage from violating the rule, so the rule feels self-evidently necessary. Personal story preferred; named-client story second; analogy as fallback.

### c. APPLICATION
> *"How can they apply this right now in their coaching business."*

Tactical, prescriptive, immediate. The viewer should leave this beat knowing what question to ask, what diagnostic to run, what worksheet to fill in. Example: *"On the sales call, ask one question: what have you already tried to solve this, and how much did you spend trying?"*

### d. RULE
> *"One line that ties it all up, and puts a pin in it before moving on to the next one."*

The closer. Repeatable. Often a stay-empty / star-stays-empty pattern. Example: *"If you find yourself hoping they will, that star stays empty."*

## 2. Three structural rules that govern the whole section

### 2a. The Setup-Framework Alignment Rule
> *Kyle: "The setup is teeing up 'be a specialist' but the 5-Star Client framework isn't really about specialisation, only 2 of the 5 criteria are. The other 3 are about client character. None of those are specialisation things. So the setup and the framework are teaching different things, which is why the section feels off."*

**Rule:** the wedge you open the body with MUST logically lead to all N criteria. If your setup teases idea X but only 2 of your 5 criteria are about X, the section will feel off — the viewer feels the misalignment without being able to name it. Either rewrite the setup to a wedge that covers all N criteria, or pick different criteria.

**The universal opener Kyle uses for criteria bodies (good vs bad client / X vs not-X):**
> *"There are two kinds of [thing] that walk into your business. The first kind [behaves well + grows you in background]. The second kind [behaves badly + shrinks you in background]. The problem is on paper they look identical. What separates them is N specific criteria you can check before you say yes."*

This works for any criteria list — substitute your thing (client, hire, prospect, partner, deal, sales call, ad creative). The N criteria are then re-framed as the diagnostic for telling the two kinds apart, not as separate lessons.

### 2b. The Analogy Test
> *Kyle: "When something doesn't click for someone watching, it creates cognitive load, which means they have to stop and think, and if they have to think, they're probably going to click off. You want the analogy to be so easy to understand that they immediately understand it, with no effort."*

**Rule:** every analogy in a script must be **immediately understood, with no effort.** If the viewer has to read or hear it twice to get it, cut it. Failure pattern: clever analogies (Lauren's original migraine-pill / ibuprofen had to be read twice — got cut). Success pattern: domain-universal analogies (athletes-with-same-coach, doctor-vs-surgeon, plumbing leak) where the source domain is something every viewer already understands.

**Audit before recording.** For each analogy, ask: *"If I dropped this analogy on a stranger at a party, would they get it inside one sentence, no follow-up needed?"* If no, swap it.

### 2c. The Growth-Dynamics Logic (for two-kinds-of-X opener)
> *Kyle: "One pays you well, does the work, gets results, sends referrals (and thus GROWS your coaching business because you get good case studies and referrals). The other drains your energy, fights every step, churns, and tells people you didn't deliver (thus makes it more difficult to GROW your coaching business)."*

**Rule:** when using the two-kinds-of-X opener, make the growth-dynamic logic EXPLICIT, not just the symptoms. Don't only say *"good clients refer / bad clients churn"* — say *"good clients grow your business in the background; bad clients shrink it in the background, and you don't see the shrinking because it's the bookings you never got."* The invisible-loss frame is what makes the rule emotionally land.

## 3. Worked example — Lauren's 5-Star Client (5 criteria, applied)

| Criterion | STATE | COST | APPLICATION | RULE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Problem you can solve | "It has to be someone whose problem you can solve." | Marathon story (personal). Lauren took a marathon client outside her women's-strength expertise; refunded him 6 months later. | "Before you say yes, ask: have I taken someone with this exact problem from where they are to where they want to be, even once?" | "If you can't reliably take them from where they are to where they want to be, that star stays empty." |
| 2 — Coachable | "They have to be coachable." | Two-athletes-same-coach analogy (no personal story → analogy). Same program, same hours; the one who did the work won. Maps to clients who blame the method when they didn't do the reps. | "Watch what they do in the lead-up to the call. Replied to messages? Engaged with form questions? Did the pre-call homework?" | "If they won't do the small things before they pay you, they won't do the big things after. That star stays empty." |
| 3 — Willing to pay | "They have to be willing to pay." | Five-friends story (personal). Took 5 friends for free; all gained weight; *"when people don't pay, they don't pay attention."* | "On the call, ask: what have you already tried to solve this, and how much did you spend trying?" | "You're not here to convince broke people to find money. That star stays empty." |
| 4 — You genuinely like them | "You have to genuinely like them." | Worst-client visualisation (analogy/imagination). The dread on your calendar. The version of yourself you have to become for that hour. | "There's no clean diagnostic. Trust your gut after the call. Refer them out if it's a no." | "If you have to talk yourself into liking someone, that star stays empty." |
| 5 — One specific avatar | "They have to fit one specific avatar." | Doctor-vs-surgeon analogy + callback to the marathon story (a story doing double duty). | "Write your offer in one sentence: 'I help [audience] with [problem] without [obstacle].'" | "If you have to bend your avatar to fit them, that star stays empty." |

**What the structure does together:** every criterion runs the same beat shape, so the viewer learns the pattern after Criterion 1 and rides it for the remaining 4. The RULE template (*"that star stays empty"*) compounds — by Criterion 5 it lands like a refrain.

## 4. Production-cue pairing — the progressive star

When teaching N criteria with this structure, give the viewer a **visual scoreboard** that progresses with each RULE. For the 5-Star Client: an empty 5-pointed star at the start of SETUP; one point fills as each criterion's RULE closes; all five filled at the end of Criterion 5 = the five-star client. This pairs the structural repetition (STATE/COST/APPLICATION/RULE) with a visual completion arc, so the viewer's brain stays engaged across all N beats.

Substitute the visual for your criteria count: 5 = pentagon points / 7 = clock face / 4 = quadrants / 3 = traffic-light bar.

## 5. Quality gate — before you record

- [ ] Does my setup wedge logically lead to **all N** criteria, or only a subset? (Setup-Framework Alignment.)
- [ ] Did I open with the two-kinds-of-X frame and make the growth-dynamics logic explicit?
- [ ] Does every criterion have STATE / COST / APPLICATION / RULE — all four beats?
- [ ] Does every COST beat have a personal story, a named-client example, OR an analogy (in that preference order)?
- [ ] Does every analogy pass the no-effort test? (Could a stranger get it inside one sentence?)
- [ ] Does every APPLICATION beat give the viewer something they can run today, in their actual business?
- [ ] Does every RULE land in one repeatable line? Does it follow the same template across all N criteria, so it compounds?
- [ ] Does the production cue pair a visual scoreboard with the structural repetition?

## 6. Common failure modes

- **Setup tees up the wrong wedge.** If your wedge only covers 2 of 5 criteria, the other 3 will feel orphaned. Fix: rewrite the wedge to a frame that owns all N (Kyle's good-vs-bad-X is the universal one).
- **Missing APPLICATION on one or more criteria.** Lauren's v6 had personal stories for criteria 1 and 3 but no APPLICATION on any of the five. Kyle's flag: *"this sub point, and the others is missing some application advice."*
- **Clever-but-opaque analogy.** Migraine-pill/ibuprofen was the failure here. Cute, but the viewer has to think → cognitive load → click off. Replace with universally-understood analogies.
- **Inconsistent beat structure across criteria.** If criterion 1 has Story + Rule, criterion 2 has just Rule, criterion 3 has Story + Application + Rule — the viewer can't ride the pattern. Pick the structure and run it uniformly across all N.
- **Stories that don't show the COST.** A COST beat must show the wreckage from breaking the rule — not just illustrate the rule. The marathon story doesn't tell you what makes a good client; it shows what happens when you take a bad-fit client (six months wasted, refund, emotional cost).
